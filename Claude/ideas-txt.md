About this collection

Educators are currently filled with rollercoaster emotions and reactions.
There is a lot of excitement and fear in the air about the use of AI in
education. A range of perspectives and positions are currently shared
openly and widely in publications and panel discussions.
This collection captures where we are at this moment in time with our
collective thinking about potential alternative uses and applications of
AI that could make a real difference and potentially create new learning,
development and opportunities for our students and educators, for all
of us. The collection is based on an open invitation to all educators and
students to share ideas on how AI tools such as ChatGPT, DALL·E 2, and
Midjourney, for example, could be used in inventive ways for learning,
teaching and scholarship.

Experimentation is at
the heart of education.
Being open to diverse ideas will help us
make novel connections that can lead to new
discoveries and insights to make a positive
contribution to our world.
Ideas shared may be in its embryonic stage,
half-baked, but worth exploring further
through active and creative inquiry.

We are mindful of the importance of critical and ethical use of AI in
education settings and more generally.
Thank you for all the contributions from authors across 19 countries:
Australia, Canada, China, Egypt, Germany, Greece, India, Israel, Italy,
Ireland, Jordan, Liberia, Mexico, South Africa, Spain, Thailand, Turkey,
United Kingdom and the US.
The collection is made available as an Open Educational Resource
(OER) via the #creativeHE website, Zenodo and further platforms. As the
collection is made available under the Creative Commons License CCBY-NC-SA, anybody can use the collection as open data to further
interrogate the use of AI in Education. Please share any resulting further
outcomes with the editorial team and the wider community.

Note: Most images in this collection were submitted as screenshots,
impacting visual resolution and legibility. We value the importance of
accessible education and hope to resolve this in future volumes.
If you would like to find out more about AI and Education and
particularly ChatGPT and Education, please access the useful guide
by Torrey Trust made available via the National Teaching Repository.
The Teachers’ prompt guide for ChatGPT by Andrew Herft will
provide some inspiration. AI, Chatbots & ChatGPT for Teachers, a
free course, may also help you.
For all those who responded to our invitation and contributed,
thank you.

– The editors.

My idea
Exploring the use of AI feedback

feedback they receive and give in
new ways. Evidence suggests that

We know that poetry and storytelling

with human input help educators

students engage in peer-to-peer

Critical and moral reasoning

Author:
Antonio Martínez-Arboleda
Contact details:
sllama@leeds.ac.uk

My idea
I wanted to explore the use of AI for
interpreting and evaluating extracts of
interviews, in order to help students
to understand the value, as well as
the limitations, of AI ChatGPT-3 as
a learning tool, in particular when it
comes to completeness of information,
critical thinking and moral reasoning.
In my non-scientific experiment, one
extract of an interview was given to
the Chat in two different threads,
one of them with greater context and
another one without much context.
The central question to the Chat was
the same for both threads: What value
does this extract have scientifically,
educationally and/or humanistically?
Moral inconsistencies, and differences
in the quality of responses of
ChatGPT-3 can be appreciated
when comparing both chats.
The conversations took place in
January and early February 2023.
What I aim to achieve
Students can now use Artificial
Intelligence to seek for answers to
questions that educators present to
them as part of their homework or in
class discussions. I intend to show how
the dialogical and critical engagement

Chapter IV

with this Chat by students is a valuable
exercise for them for the development
of their own critical thinking. In more
practical terms, I would like to suggest
that purposeful, meaningful and
timely framing and contextualisation
of students’ questions to the Chat,
coupled by a solid moral compass,
are essential for the responsible use
of this Chat.
Where the inspiration comes from
I was invited to give a keynote speech
in a conference on Artificial Intelligence,
Data Science and Text Analytics for
Education Resources on 10/1/23
(“Open Educational Practice for Open
Research in Student Education and
beyond”). In preparation for my talk,
I decided to ask the Chat the same
questions that I ask my own students
in one of our class activities, when
they are introduced to the analysis
of interviews, which is the research
data that they produce and work
with. My dialogues with the Chat in
two different threads were full of
serendipity,
as
I
learned
unexpected qualities and flaws
of this AI tool. I was awarded a
prize for this talk, organised at the
University of Leeds and supported by
the Alan Turing Institute.

AI-powered persona-generating
program for student researchers

Author:

Institution/organisation:

Soroush Sabbaghan

Werklund School of Education,
University of Calgary

Contact details:
ssabbagh@ucalgary.ca

Context:
Higher Education, Students

Role:
Educator & Researcher

Tool(s) used:
OpenAI API

My idea
My project involves developing a
graphical user interface for a program
that employs artificial intelligence (AI)
to impersonate persona given by the
user so that a human researcher can
interview it.
What I aim to achieve
With this bot, a student-researcher
can ask the AI-powered virtual
participant questions on a variety of
topics, including opinions on, general
and domain-specific issues. Based
on the information provided, the
AI-powered participant will respond in
a manner that is indistinguishable from
normal human-to-human interactions.
It would be as if you are interviewing a
real person through a textual medium
rather than an audio/visual one.
The interaction can be downloaded

Chapter IV

and used as “authentic” data for
qualitative analysis in a pedagogical
context.
Where the inspiration comes from
I believe students will learn how
to conduct thematic, discourse,
content, and narrative analysis better
if they have authentic data. However,
any human interaction for research
purposes is subject to ethics review.
The implications of this technology
are that our students could conduct
research involving “humans” without
needing to obtain ethical approval
and still go through an experiential
learning process. Students can also
use this technology to test the efficacy
of their data-gathering questions by
conducting interviews with various
AI-powered participants, enabling
them to develop more effective datagathering tools.

Using AI to develop variety in
scenario-based assessments

Author:
Samuel Saunders
Contact details:
samuel.saunders@liverpool.ac.uk
Role:

My idea
Much of the discourse surrounding
auto-generative tools, particularly
OpenAI’s ChatGPT, has focused on
how students can use it to create
content in response to a given
assessment brief - with associated
concerns about academic integrity
naturally following quickly (King &
ChatGPT, 2023). But academics can
also use the technology to help create
authentic, meaningful, and scenariobased assessments. Academics
can, and should, explore the idea
of inputting (or, indeed, getting their
students to input) the parameters of
their assessments into tools, such
as ChatGPT, and ask it to create a
real-world brief by acting as a ‘client’
in the context of their disciplinary
background. In the example on the
right, ChatGPT was asked to act
as a client to a marketing business
and create a brief for a marketing
expert to design a campaign for an
unidentified product, including a
budget, timescale and market reach.
ChatGPT responded with a request for

Chapter IV

a 6-month, $100,000 campaign across
the USA to sell smart thermostats.
Students could then work to create
materials for this campaign as an
assessment task.

Educational Developer

What I aim to achieve

Higher education,
Summative assessment,
Authentic assessment(s).

There is potential here for variety
in assessment, which reduces the
potential for copying and academic
misconduct and increases the
inclusivity of the assessment. If
students input the assessment
parameters themselves, it creates a
tailor-made task for themselves, and
ChatGPT responds with a different
scenario each time it is asked to
produce one. It also helps drive up
the authenticity and applicability of
the assessment.

My idea
Utilise ChatGPT (or a similar platform)
as a host/facilitator simulating a
television quiz format that students
are familiar with. This might be a
popular series in their region, suitable
for delivery in a small group format.
Working examples include: Who
Wants to Be a Millionaire/Kaun
Banega Crorepati [UK/India] or Family
Feud/100 Mexicanos Dijeron/以一敌百
[USA/Mexico/China]. The format and
content of each quiz can be refined or
adapted as required through prompts,
for example, to support the number
of students or tweak the mode of
engagement (scored questions,
round elimination, first to answer,
etc.). Team-based participation is also
possible. Unfamiliar quiz formats can
also be explained to the platform. In
ChatGPT’s words:
“As the host of a TV quiz show for a small
group of students, I would provide a set of
questions related to a specific subject or topic.
Each student would then write down their
answers. I could facilitate this type of virtual
quiz show by collecting and revealing answers
and scores through text input. The students
would simply need to communicate answers
to me, and I would then be able to keep
track of who is in the lead or who has been
eliminated.”
